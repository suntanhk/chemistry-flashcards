01

Chirality (Optical Isomerism)

What makes a carbon chiral?

A carbon atom is a chiral centre (stereocentre) when it is bonded to four different groups. Nothing else about the carbon matters — not its position in the chain, not whether it looks "special."

Fig 1. A chiral centre bonded to four different groups (OH, H, CH3, C2H5). Swapping two groups generates the mirror-image enantiomer — solid wedge = towards you, dashed line = away from you.

Enantiomers

  • Definition: stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other.
  • Same bonds, same molecular formula, same melting/boiling point, same reactivity with achiral reagents.
  • Differ only in how they rotate plane-polarised light — this is called optical activity.
Fig 2. A polarimeter set-up: plane-polarised light passing through a chiral sample is rotated by a specific angle — clockwise (+) or anticlockwise (–).

Racemic mixtures

  • A 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers is called a racemate.
  • The (+) rotation from one enantiomer is exactly cancelled by the (–) rotation of the other.
  • Net result: optically inactive — no observable rotation at all.
Exam trap
A carbon with only two different groups (and two identical ones) is not a chiral centre. Check all four substituents individually before deciding — don't assume from a quick glance.
A* tip
"No optical activity observed" has two possible explanations: (1) the molecule is achiral, or (2) it's chiral but present as a racemic mixture. Mark schemes reward giving both when the question doesn't rule one out.
02

Carbonyls (Aldehydes & Ketones)

Structure

Fig 3. Aldehyde: C=O bonded to one H and one R group. Ketone: C=O bonded to two carbon chains (R and R'), never to H.

Naming

  • Aldehydes: suffix -al (ethanal, propanal). The carbonyl carbon is always C1 — no locant needed.
  • Ketones: suffix -one, with a locant number (butan-2-one, pentan-3-one).

Distinguishing tests

TestPositive resultWhat it tells you
Tollens' reagent (warm)Silver mirror formsAldehyde present (ketone: no reaction)
Fehling's solution (warm)Blue → brick-red precipitateAldehyde present (ketone: stays blue)
2,4-DNPHOrange/yellow precipitateA carbonyl (C=O) is present — either type
Triiodomethane (iodoform)Pale yellow precipitate, antiseptic smellCH3CO– group present (methyl ketone)

Why the difference? Aldehydes have an H atom directly on the carbonyl carbon, which can be oxidised (removed) to form a carboxylic acid. Ketones have no such H — both remaining bonds go to carbon chains — so they resist mild oxidation.

Reduction

  • Reducing agent: NaBH4 — provides a hydride ion (H⁻) as the nucleophile.
  • Aldehyde + NaBH4 → primary alcohol
  • Ketone + NaBH4 → secondary alcohol
A* tip
Reduction of an unsymmetrical ketone by NaBH4 can also give a chiral product — and, exactly as with HCN below, hydride can attack the planar carbonyl carbon from either face, so the product is a racemic mixture too.
03

The Link: Nucleophilic Addition of HCN

This is where the two topics fuse into one synoptic exam question — the most heavily tested combination of chirality and carbonyl chemistry.

Fig 4. The carbonyl carbon is flat (trigonal planar), so the CN⁻ nucleophile can attack from either face with equal probability — this is the entire basis of the racemic-mixture explanation.

Mechanism steps

  1. Step 1: CN⁻ (nucleophile) attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon using its lone pair. The C=O π bond breaks; electrons move onto the oxygen, forming an alkoxide intermediate (O⁻).
  2. Step 2: The O⁻ intermediate picks up an H⁺ (from HCN or H2O) to give the final –OH group.

Product: a hydroxynitrile, containing both –OH and –C≡N groups.

Exam trap
Curly arrows must start from a lone pair or a bond — never from a charge symbol and never from an atom label. On CN⁻, the arrow starts from the lone pair on carbon, not on nitrogen.

Why use HCN + NaCN together?

Pure HCN is a very weak acid and barely dissociates, so there's little free CN⁻ available. Adding NaCN supplies a ready source of CN⁻ nucleophile, while HCN still supplies the H⁺ needed in step 2.

A* model sentence — memorise this
"The product is optically inactive because the carbonyl carbon is planar, so the nucleophile attacks with equal probability from either face, producing equal amounts of both enantiomers — a racemic mixture."

This explanation only applies when the carbonyl is unsymmetrical (R ≠ R'). If R = R' (e.g. propanone), the product carbon ends up with two identical groups and is not chiral at all.

Key Takeaways

  • Chiral centre = carbon with four different groups. Check every carbon systematically.
  • Enantiomers = non-superimposable mirror images; identical properties except rotation of plane-polarised light.
  • Racemic mixture = 50:50 enantiomers → optically inactive (rotations cancel).
  • Aldehyde vs ketone: Tollens'/Fehling's distinguish them (aldehyde positive); 2,4-DNPH confirms either; iodoform confirms CH3CO–.
  • NaBH4 reduces: aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol.
  • HCN nucleophilic addition → hydroxynitrile; on an unsymmetrical carbonyl, gives a racemic mixture because attack occurs equally from both faces of the planar carbon.
  • Full-mark racemic explanation needs FOUR parts: planar carbon → equal-probability attack → equal enantiomer amounts → optically inactive racemate.
