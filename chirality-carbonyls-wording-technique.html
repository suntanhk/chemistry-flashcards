EDEXCEL 9CH0 · COMBINED TOPIC TEST · 9 OCTOBER

Own Words vs A* Terminology

Closing the gap between knowing the content and scoring for it

Why this matters

Examiners mark against a fixed list of acceptable phrases — not against "does the student seem to understand." An answer that is scientifically correct in everyday language often gets 0 marks if it doesn't contain the specific term or relationship the mark scheme is looking for.

Every pair below shows a realistic "own words" answer next to the exact phrasing that scores the mark — study the bolded key terms closely, since those are usually the exact words the mark scheme checks for.

Rule 1: Name the group, don't describe it. "The OH thing" → "hydroxyl group."
Rule 2: State the comparison word. "Different" isn't enough — say "four different groups."
Rule 3: Use the defined term, not a synonym. "Mirror twins" → "enantiomers."
Rule 4: Give the mechanism vocabulary. "Attacks" needs "nucleophile," "δ+," "curly arrow," "lone pair."

1. Chirality — Definitions & Identification

Q: What makes a carbon atom a chiral centre?
✗ Own words
"It's a carbon that has different things attached to it so it's not symmetrical."
Loses marks: vague — "different things" isn't quantified, and "not symmetrical" is not the accepted definition.
✓ Exam wording
"A carbon atom bonded to four different groups."
Q: Define enantiomers.
✗ Own words
"They're two versions of a molecule that look the same but are actually opposite, like your hands."
Loses marks: "look the same but opposite" is imprecise; the hand analogy is fine for understanding but is not itself a mark-scheme phrase.
✓ Exam wording
"Stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other."
Q: Two structures are drawn. State the relationship between them.
✗ Own words
"They're basically flipped versions of the same thing."
Loses marks: "flipped" does not specify mirror-image relationship, and doesn't name the isomer type.
✓ Exam wording
"They are enantiomers — a pair of optical isomers."

2. Optical Activity & Racemic Mixtures

Q: Explain why a racemic mixture does not rotate plane-polarised light.
✗ Own words
"Because there's equal amounts of both so they cancel each other out."
Loses marks: doesn't state WHAT is equal (the two enantiomers) or WHAT is cancelling (the rotations, not the molecules).
✓ Exam wording
"It contains equal amounts of both enantiomers; the clockwise and anticlockwise rotations are equal and opposite, so they cancel out."
Q: A polarimeter shows no rotation for a known chiral sample. Suggest why.
✗ Own words
"Maybe the two types are mixed together evenly."
Loses marks: "the two types" is not named; "mixed together evenly" is not the term "racemic mixture."
✓ Exam wording
"The sample is a racemic mixture — a 50:50 mixture of both enantiomers — which is optically inactive."
Q: Describe how a polarimeter is used.
✗ Own words
"You shine special light through the chemical and see which way it bends."
Loses marks: light does not "bend" — the PLANE of the light rotates. "Special light" should be named.
✓ Exam wording
"Plane-polarised light is passed through the sample; the angle and direction through which the plane is rotated is measured."

3. Carbonyl Structure & Distinguishing Tests

Q: State the structural difference between an aldehyde and a ketone.
✗ Own words
"An aldehyde has the double bond O at the end and a ketone has it in the middle."
Loses marks: "double bond O" should be "carbonyl group / C=O"; "at the end/middle" should reference the carbon chain position, and what the carbon is bonded to.
✓ Exam wording
"In an aldehyde the carbonyl group is at the end of the carbon chain, bonded to one hydrogen atom; in a ketone it is within the chain, bonded to two carbon-containing groups."
Q: Describe the result of warming an aldehyde with Tollens' reagent.
✗ Own words
"It goes shiny/silver."
Loses marks: "goes shiny" is not the accepted observation — the mark scheme requires "silver mirror."
✓ Exam wording
"A silver mirror forms on the inside of the test tube."
Q: Describe the result of warming an aldehyde with Fehling's solution.
✗ Own words
"It turns red."
Loses marks: colour AND state must both be given — "red" alone misses "brick-red" and "precipitate" (a solid, not just a colour change).
✓ Exam wording
"The blue solution forms a brick-red precipitate."
Q: State what a positive 2,4-DNPH test shows.
✗ Own words
"It shows there's a carbonyl thing in it and it goes orange."
Loses marks: "carbonyl thing" should be "carbonyl group (C=O)"; result should be "orange/yellow precipitate," not just "goes orange."
✓ Exam wording
"An orange/yellow precipitate forms, confirming the presence of a carbonyl group."
Q: Explain why ketones cannot be oxidised in the same way as aldehydes.
✗ Own words
"Because ketones don't have the right atom to lose."
Loses marks: "the right atom" must be named as hydrogen, and its exact position must be stated.
✓ Exam wording
"Aldehydes have a hydrogen atom bonded directly to the carbonyl carbon, which can be removed during oxidation; ketones have no hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon, so they resist oxidation."

4. Reduction with NaBH4

Q: State the product of reducing an aldehyde with NaBH4.
✗ Own words
"It becomes an alcohol."
Loses marks: "an alcohol" is not specific enough — the mark scheme requires the CLASS of alcohol.
✓ Exam wording
"A primary alcohol."
Q: Name the type of reaction occurring when NaBH4 reduces a carbonyl.
✗ Own words
"NaBH4 adds hydrogen to it."
Loses marks: describes WHAT happens but not the reaction TYPE, which is what's usually asked.
✓ Exam wording
"Nucleophilic addition (reduction) — the hydride ion, H⁻, acts as the nucleophile."

5. HCN Nucleophilic Addition Mechanism

Q: Explain why the carbonyl carbon is attacked by CN⁻.
✗ Own words
"Because the carbon is kind of positive so the CN wants to go there."
Loses marks: "kind of positive" should be δ+; "wants to go there" should reference electrostatic attraction and the term nucleophile.
✓ Exam wording
"The carbonyl carbon is δ+ (electrophilic) because oxygen is more electronegative; CN⁻ is a nucleophile and is attracted to this electron-deficient carbon."
Q: Describe the curly arrow from CN⁻ in step 1 of the mechanism.
✗ Own words
"The arrow comes from the minus sign on CN and points at the carbon."
Loses marks: this is a classic examiner trap — arrows NEVER start from a charge symbol. This exact error is heavily penalised.
✓ Exam wording
"The curly arrow starts from the lone pair on the carbon atom of CN⁻ and points to the δ+ carbonyl carbon."
Q: Describe what happens to the C=O bond during the mechanism.
✗ Own words
"The double bond breaks and goes onto the oxygen."
Loses marks: should specify it is the π bond (not the whole double bond), and name the resulting species.
✓ Exam wording
"The C=O π bond breaks; the electrons move onto the oxygen, forming an alkoxide intermediate (O⁻)."
Q: [The big one] Explain fully why the HCN addition product is a racemic mixture. (4 marks)
✗ Own words
"Because the CN can attack from both sides so you get a mix of both and it doesn't rotate the light."
Loses marks: this answer is conceptually correct but only scores ~1–2 of 4 marks. It never says "planar," never says "equal probability," and never names "racemic mixture" or "optically inactive" explicitly.
✓ Exam wording — 4 separate marking points
"① The carbonyl carbon is planar (trigonal planar / sp²).
② CN⁻ can attack from either face with equal probability.
③ This produces equal amounts of both enantiomers.
④ The mixture is a racemate, which is optically inactive because the rotations cancel."

6. General Command-Word Traps

Q: "Explain" vs "State" vs "Describe" — command word confusion.
✗ Own words habit
Answering every command word the same way — usually just stating a fact, even when "explain" or "describe" is asked.
Loses marks: "state" needs one fact; "describe" needs an observation/process in sequence; "explain" needs a reason (often "because…" linking cause to effect) — under-answering an "explain" question caps the marks even if the fact given is correct.
✓ Exam approach
"State" → give the fact only (e.g. a name/product). "Describe" → give the observation or steps in order. "Explain" → give the reason, usually including a causal link ("…because…", "…due to…").
Q: Naming a compound in an answer.
✗ Own words
"The alcohol thing" / "the OH one" / "the product with the CN in it."
Loses marks: vague descriptions of a compound are not accepted where the IUPAC name or structural formula is expected.
✓ Exam wording
Always give the full IUPAC name (e.g. "propan-1-ol," "2-hydroxypropanenitrile") or a clear structural/skeletal formula.

3 habits to build before the 9th

  • Before writing an answer, ask: "Is there a named term for this concept?" If yes, use it — don't paraphrase it.
  • For any "explain" question worth 3+ marks, write in separate numbered/bulleted points, not one flowing sentence — it forces you to hit each marking point distinctly.
  • After finishing a mock paper, go back through every answer and circle any phrase that isn't a "textbook" term — then look up and memorise the correct term for next time.
